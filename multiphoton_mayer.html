The theory behind this page

Einstein, 1905

One photon hands all of its energy hf to one electron. Emission needs hf ≥ φ, the work function. At ordinary light intensities, a frequency below the threshold frees nothing, however bright the lamp.

Goeppert Mayer, 1931

In her doctoral thesis in Göttingen she showed that quantum theory also allows an atom or electron to take up two photons in a single elementary act, passing through a virtual intermediate state that lasts only about a femtosecond. The rate grows as I², so it only becomes noticeable at very high intensity, which lasers later made possible.

The general rule

For n photons absorbed together: n · hf ≥ φ, KEmax = n · hf − φ, and the rate scales as Iⁿ. Setting n = 1 gives back Einstein's law, so the classic effect is the first term of a larger series. Two-photon cross-sections are measured in GM units (10⁻⁵⁰ cm⁴ s per photon), named in her honour.

Modelling note: the yield here is taken as (I / 10¹² W/cm²)ⁿ, an illustrative scaling that keeps the Iⁿ dependence. Real coefficients depend on the material and the light pulse. The animation compresses the probability on a log scale so that rare events can still be watched.
